## What a passphrase is

A passphrase is an extra **passcode** you add on top of your seed words — **a second lock**.

There is only one rule:

> Same seed words + a different passphrase = a completely different wallet.

That means two things:

1. If you type the wrong passphrase, the wallet won't complain — it simply opens a new, empty wallet.
2. Lose the passphrase, and you lose the coins.

## It's easier to crack than you think

The BIP39 standard says that deriving private keys from a passphrase uses only **2048 rounds** of PBKDF2.

2048 is deliberately small — so that an underpowered hardware wallet can finish the computation in about a second. The price: once an attacker knows / guesses your seed words, testing candidate passphrases is just as cheap. An ordinary graphics card can make **tens of thousands** of complete guesses per second.

| Passphrase type | Rough entropy | After the attacker gets your seed words |
|---|---|---|
| Initials + birthday | ~20 bits | Cracked within minutes |
| A song lyric, movie line, or famous quote | ~25 bits | Already in dictionaries — just as fast |
| 4 random words | ~44 bits | A determined attacker can afford it |
| 6 random words | ~66 bits | Safe with the computing power that exists today |

So don't tell yourself that a somewhat complex passphrase makes a low-entropy seed safe — **our main defense is still the seed words.**

**The seed words are the main lock, the big lock; the passphrase is the auxiliary lock, the small lock.**

> **Note:** I hesitated about writing that — strictly speaking it isn't accurate, since a passphrase can easily be set with far more entropy than the seed words. But I do want to warn the people who think "with a somewhat complex passphrase, I can safely keep serious money on seed words generated by a phone wallet": high-entropy seed words are our main line of defense.

## How we use a passphrase

So the real question is: who are you defending against?

Your seed words were **generated with dice** and have never touched the internet — brute-forcing them would cost the energy of a universe. The realistic scenarios are:

1. **People who visit your home** — relatives, friends, roommates, cleaners, repair workers, movers — who catch sight of your backup;
2. **The $5 wrench attack** — someone coerces you in person to hand over the wallet.

So you're not fighting a GPU farm — you're fighting **the imagination of people who know you**. That changes the design goal:

> You don't need to beat the world's computing power, only the guesses of people who know you — so the design should lean toward "memorable".

The popular advice of "6 or more random words" is a configuration for fighting GPU farms. The cost: you can never remember it, so you have to store it somewhere — and storing it is a new risk and burden. The cleaner your seed words (rolled with dice, never online), the more entitled you are to make the passphrase easy to remember.

My advice:

1. Do use a passphrase — and keep **no coins under the bare seed words**. (After the 2026 Coldcard incident: keeping a small "sentinel" amount under the bare seed words is like hanging a "nothing valuable here" sign on your door — it only tells a hacker there *is* treasure behind it, and that your passphrase is worth spending real computing power to crack.)
2. Make sure the seed words — your main line of defense — were rolled with dice; then design the passphrase on the principle that you'll never forget it. You can use a password you already rely on every day, such as an everyday app or bank-card password, as the passphrase — and rehearse a recovery every now and then, because anything left unused for long enough is guaranteed to be forgotten.

## Passphrase checklist

- **Case-sensitive, and spaces count.** `Kiwi iron` and `kiwi  iron` are two different wallets. During the handover period, copy your notes exactly as written.
- **A wrong passphrase won't error out.** One character off and the wallet happily opens a brand-new empty wallet. So when "my balance is gone," don't panic — nine times out of ten the passphrase was mistyped. Replay it character by character and try again.
- **Store it apart from the seed words.** Whoever holds both holds the coins; writing them together is as good as having no second lock.
- **Rehearse first, fund later.** After setting a passphrase, do a full recovery drill with a small amount: wipe the wallet, restore from memory and your backup, confirm the spelling, and only then move serious funds in.

> Plenty of people like to base their passphrase on "initials + birthday". Please don't.
