## The real goal of a backup

> Your coins = 12/24 words + a passphrase

The quality of your backup directly equals the security level of your assets.

Complexity is the #1 enemy for most people — far bigger than the threat of hackers. From my experience, for most people a backup mainly defends against:

1. **being casually seen by relatives and friends;**
2. **a single point of failure** (the backup gets damp or damaged).

So don't make the backup too complicated: copy the words into two notebooks and keep them in different places. For most people, that's already enough.

## Four iron rules

1. **Seed words never touch the internet.** Don't print them (printers have caches and cloud sync), don't photograph them, don't screenshot them, don't type them into any networked device.
2. **Keep seed words and passphrase apart.** The main lock is the seed words, and the passphrase is the secondary lock. Written together, it's as if you only ever set one lock — but lose either one, and you permanently lose the coins.
3. **Multiple copies, multiple places.** Make 2–3 copies and keep them in different places. A single fire or burglary at home only costs you one of them.
4. **Rehearse recovery.** Human memory is unreliable. In the early days, restore your wallet from the backup once a month; later, once a year — otherwise, in time, you'll forget how.

> If your seed words were generated with dice and stored offline, the line of defense is already solid — there's no need to design a passphrase so complex that you forget it. In that case, keeping the passphrase in a cloud note can be an acceptable trade-off. Some people would advise this; assess whether it suits your own situation. Whichever storage method you choose, don't describe your actual storage habits in public.
